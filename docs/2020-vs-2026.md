# 2020 vs 2026: the same question, built twice

The question hasn't changed: which passages of Hume's *Treatise* does the scholarship on personal
identity lean on, and which papers find their way to passages the rest of the field passes by? I
answered it first in 2020, by hand, and again in 2026 with Claude agents doing most of the
building. Both versions are online, so you can compare them directly:

- 2020: [treatise-mapping-project-ur.outsidethevillage.com](https://treatise-mapping-project-ur.outsidethevillage.com),
  code in [Hume-on-Personal-Identity](https://github.com/mdpriselac/Hume-on-Personal-Identity)
- 2026: [treatise-mapping-project-new.outsidethevillage.com](https://treatise-mapping-project-new.outsidethevillage.com),
  code in this repository

## What stayed the same

The definition of the literature: the PhilPapers leaf on Hume on Personal Identity, 143
publications in June 2020, of which I could get 101 in a usable digital form. The map of the text:
the Hume Texts Online edition, 1,375 numbered paragraphs, with the Selby-Bigge/Nidditch to Norton
concordance tables I built in April 2020. And the scoring idea: every citation is worth one point,
spread over the paragraphs it names.

## Extraction

In 2020 the parser worked down a ladder of citation forms, from Norton numbers to SBN pages to
marked page references, and a looser "aggressive" search added bare page numbers. When I reviewed
it in 2026, the biggest problem was plain: **38 of the 100 processed papers registered no strict
citations at all.** The code stripped every space from the PDF text before searching, three PDFs
were scans with no text layer, and many papers cite a bare page number like (261) after a
quotation, which only the loose search caught. The loose search, in turn, counted page numbers
that weren't Hume's.

The 2026 pipeline:

1. Converts every PDF again with docling, using OCR only for the three scans.
2. Detects citations with a pattern detector that keeps the 2020 ladder but fixes two forms it
   got wrong: a Norton cite followed by its SBN page was counted twice, and Penelhum's
   `T 1. iv. 6. 261` style was missed.
3. Checks the uncertain candidates, mostly bare page numbers after quotations. Where the quoted
   words match a passage in the *Treatise*, the page number is tested against that passage
   directly. Only what remains goes to a model. On a sample of 200 candidates, the model check was
   right about nine times in ten, and on the 33 candidates that also had a quote match it agreed
   every time. Most of the uncertain candidates turned out to be page cites to other scholars'
   books, which the 2020 loose rule would have counted as Hume.
4. Reads by hand the papers in which the detector still found nothing.

| | 2020 (strict) | 2026 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Citations | about 2,756 | 3,308 |
| Papers with no usable citations | 38 | 17 |
| Papers with enough citations to compare (10 or more) | 46 | 56 |

Every 2026 citation is stored with its source (pattern, quote match, model check or read by hand),
the words around it, and the paragraphs it maps to, so each number on the new site traces back to
a sentence in a paper.

## Measures

The 2020 site ranked papers by their "difference from the literature", a distance between
percentage profiles. In 2026 I found that it, TF-IDF and KL divergence all ranked off-topic papers
(on justice, or substance) as the most novel. That isn't what I meant by novelty. The new site
uses two axes instead:

- **Focus:** a paper's share of citations on the 15 most-cited paragraphs.
- **Surprise:** how unlikely its rarest precise citation is, given how much the paper cites.

Nine of the 56 comparable papers are both focused and surprising, among them Robison 1974 on
necessary connexion. Turned around, 47 paragraphs are cited by three papers or fewer, at least one
of them focused: the candidate "novel passages" the project set out to find in 2020.

Grouping papers on their whole profile produced one giant cluster, because nearly everyone cites
1.4.6 and the Appendix. Setting those aside revealed four themes: the self and the passions,
causation and the Appendix problem, body and the external world, and the immateriality of the
soul.

## Who did what

In 2020 I did everything myself: assembling the corpus, building the concordance, writing the
parser, scoring and comparison code, and the website.

In 2026 Claude agents wrote nearly all of the new code and ran the pipeline, from plans I set and
reviewed. The questions about what to fix came from reviewing the 2020 work: why so many papers
had no citations, and why the novelty ranking put off-topic papers on top. The decisions about
what counts as a citation, which measures to trust, and whether the themes were worth keeping were
mine. The agents' commits carry a `Co-Authored-By: Claude` trailer.

What I take from the comparison is that the hard part didn't move. In 2020 it was knowing how Hume
scholars have cited the *Treatise* since the 1950s well enough to write a parser for it. In 2026
it was knowing which of the model's verdicts to trust, and building the checks, like the quote
match, that let me find out.
